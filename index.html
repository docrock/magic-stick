Parts list · Creators’ Sandbox

The Magic
StickOne stand, no excuses

The reason you didn't shoot that content wasn't a shortage of ideas. It was friction. Most people don't quit creating, they quit setting up. So here is the whole rig: one stand, four things bolted to it, and nothing to tear down when you're done.

Total carry weight 22 lb Hands needed One Setup time Zero, it stays built

Start here

You only need four things

Everything below is just options for these four jobs. Buy none of it if you already own something that does the job. Mix, match, and use what's in the closet. That is the entire point.

  1. 01

    Something to shoot with

    Any camera. A mirrorless body, a pocket gimbal, the phone in your hand. It just needs a quick release plate on the bottom.

  2. 02

    Something to be heard

    A small mic. Wired if you can, so you always have a clean reference track to sync against.

  3. 03

    Something to see

    A monitor big enough to actually read, so you stop talking to the flippy screen instead of the lens.

  4. 04

    Something for light

    One small panel gets you most of the way. Bigger room, bigger light, but start with one.

Nothing here is required. These are the exact pieces on my stand plus well reviewed alternates at different budgets. Every category has a cheap way in. Read what the part does, then go find your version of it.

How things attach

Off the wall, on a battery

Now the outside version. There are two honest paths here and the right one depends on whether you fly with your gear. An NP-F battery is the light, cheap way in. A mini V-mount is the sweet spot, because 99Wh is more capacity than an NP-F and still legal in your carry on.

The airline rule, since this is the thing people get wrong at the gate. Lithium batteries up to 100Wh fly in carry on with no approval needed. Between 100Wh and 160Wh you need airline approval. Above that they do not fly at all. Every mini V-mount on this list is 99Wh or under on purpose. The power stations further down are 288Wh, which means they are car and campsite power, not plane power.

Job two

Something to be heard

People will forgive a soft picture. They will not forgive bad audio. I like to be hardwired when I can, if only because it gives me a clean reference track to sync everything else against.

The plug thing that ruins recordings. Cameras want TRS, which has two black rings on the plug. Phones want TRRS, which has three. They fit each other perfectly and then give you silence or one dead channel. If you buy a mic and get nothing, this is almost always why.

The part that makes it hands free

Remotes, cables and the phone

Once the rig is built you should never have to walk to it. Your phone or tablet works as a remote through the camera maker's own app, and Fuji, Canon, Sony, Lumix and DJI all have a decent one. If you would rather press a real button, here is what actually works.

The remote trap, and it catches almost everybody. Most cheap Bluetooth "camera remotes" on Amazon are phone shutter buttons. They pair to a phone as a keyboard and send a volume key. They have no idea a real camera exists and will never fire a mirrorless body. A genuine one names your specific camera in the listing.

And a Fuji specific catch: the mic jack and the wired remote jack are the same hole on these bodies, so you cannot run a wired mic and a wired remote at the same time. On the X-M5 that makes Bluetooth the right answer, not a preference.

The cable, and the correction I owe you

I said "HDMI mini, not micro" in the video and I had that backwards. Both the X-M5 and the X100VI take micro HDMI, Type D. It is on Fujifilm's own spec sheet. So if you followed along and bought a mini cable, it will not fit, and that one is on me.

The sizes go Type A full size, then Type C mini, then Type D micro, smallest last. Look at your own camera before you order, because the three get called "mini" in conversation all the time and the plugs are not interchangeable.

If you read while you shoot

Teleprompter, honestly

I mentioned running a tablet into the second HDMI input as a prompter, and that works for reading notes. I want to be straight with you about its limit though, because it is the same problem I was complaining about with the flippy screen.

A screen next to the lens is not a teleprompter. If the words sit beside the camera, your eyes sit beside the camera, and everybody watching can tell. A real prompter uses a piece of angled glass to float the text directly on the lens axis, so you read and make eye contact at the same time. If you only need bullet points, the second input is fine. If you are reading full script, you want the glass.

Why it works

The weight budget

This is the number that decides whether a rig gets used or stays in the corner. If you can pick the whole thing up with one hand and walk it to another room, you will actually shoot. If it takes two trips, you won't.

The RB400 is rated to hold about 22 lb of kit on its own, and the full build lands right around 11 lb of gear. That headroom is deliberate. It means the stand is never the thing under strain, and you can add a second camera or a bigger light later without rethinking the whole rig.

The tradeoff worth knowing: rolling C stands are easier to move because you never lift them, but they need roughly 20 inches of floor. My room is small, so I take the lift over the footprint. Big room, go wheels.
